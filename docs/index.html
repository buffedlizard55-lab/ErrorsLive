How will the scorer rule it?

A batted ball leaves the bat with a measurable exit velocity (EV), launch angle (LA), distance, trajectory and contact hardness. Between contact and the official ruling, the scorer judges “ordinary effort” (MLB's official error definition). This page runs our model on those five Statcast inputs and returns the scoring-likelihood numbers a live pipeline can read straight off the MLB Stats API feed.

Read this first — what the number can and cannot do. Across the 1,258 labelled batted balls the model was fitted on, P(error) never rises above 6.5/100, and out-of-fold the model never nominates “error” as its most likely ruling — 0 times out of 1,258. So this is a ranking tool, not a verdict: use it to decide which batted ball deserves a second look, not to declare a call. Why a hit-vs-error ruling matters — the RBI stakes

A runner on 2nd or 3rd whose run scores because of a misplay produces no RBI if the play is ruled an error — but the batter keeps the RBI if it's a hit, and a run-scoring fielder's choice can still earn one. MLB's official glossary is explicit: batters do not receive RBIs for runs that would not have scored without the help of an error; and no RBI when the run scores as a result of an error or a ground into double play. Flip one scorer's judgment on a borderline grounder and you change a batting line, a fantasy scoreboard, an arbitration number. Full rule walkthrough with verbatim citations →

What the model actually found

Loading model.json…
The one robust, useful finding. Errors concentrate hard on ground balls: 20 of the 24 ruled errors were ground balls, and ground balls are 42.3% of all batted balls in the sample — a 6.8× lift over the 0.55% error rate of every other trajectory. That is the signal the model is really picking up, and it is consistent with the physics: a hard grounder is the one batted ball that demands a clean pickup and a clean throw in the same motion.

What it is not: everything that happens after the ball leaves the bat — fielder positioning, hang time, who was playing where, and the scorer's own judgement. Those are outside the five Statcast inputs, which is why the score tops out around 6/100 instead of spanning 0–100.

Methods & audit

Where every number comes from, how the 24 games were verified against the league's own linescore endpoint, and what is flagged as unverified.

Own the Outcome — applied as project policy: every number on this site is computed in this repo (tools/*.py) from feed JSONs whose final scores were re-verified one by one against the league's official linescore endpoint (verification ledger). Nothing is copied from a secondary source, nothing is estimated by hand, and every irregularity we found is published rather than smoothed over (limitations). If a value can't be verified, it doesn't ship — and where we couldn't verify something, we say so instead of quietly estimating.
